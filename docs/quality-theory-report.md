# Quality Theory Report

## Core Definition

Organizational quality is the capability of an organization to define the value it intends to create, define the losses it must avoid, and repeatedly make decisions that stay within those boundaries under uncertainty.

Quality is not the amount of output produced. Documents, reviews, tests, inspections, and audits can be useful evidence, but they are not quality itself.

## Working Formula

Quality exists when four conditions are present:

1. Value intent is explicit enough to guide decisions.
2. Loss boundaries are explicit enough to constrain decisions.
3. Evidence is strong enough to support a verdict.
4. The organization can learn and revise the intent when context changes.

## Implications

- A page count is not quality. It may be evidence of coverage only when linked to a stakeholder need or risk.
- A review count is not quality. It may be evidence of scrutiny only when the reviewed risks and findings are traceable.
- A test count is not quality. It may be evidence of verification only when tied to loss boundaries and decision criteria.
- Veteran judgment is not automatically untrustworthy, but it must be externalized through cases, cues, exceptions, and verdict rationales.
- Quality standards may be absent. In that case, quality must be discovered from decisions, exceptions, operational behavior, and expert case adjudication.

## Quality Intent

A Quality Intent is a structured statement of what quality means in a specific organizational context.

It connects:

- stakeholder value
- risk or loss boundary
- knowledge source
- evidence
- confidence
- verdict
- governance owner

This makes quality discoverable, explainable, reviewable, and machine-checkable.
